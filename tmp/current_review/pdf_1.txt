1. Abstract 
Methanation is a specialized catalytic process in which carbon monoxide CO and hydrogen H2 are 
reacted over a catalyst in order to synthesize methane. The objectives of this experiment are to 
synthesize a nickel-based catalyst via co -precipitation and evaluate its performance parameters for CO2 
conversion and selectivity toward CH4 relative to carbon dioxide CO2. A fixed-bed tubular reactor was 
used as the apparatus to conduct the methanation reaction in this experiment, supplemented by gas 
chromatography (GC) for output analysis. The catalyst synthesis process was executed keeping a metal 
oxide ratio of 5:1.7 Al2O3: NiO by dissolving 11.832 g of nickel nitrate hexahydrate and 65.87 g of 
aluminum nitrate nonahydrate , utilizing 130 mL of a 7N potassium hydroxide KOH solution  to 
maintain a neutral pH and ensure uniform dispersion of metal ions. A total of 12 grams of catalyst was 
synthesized , filtered, and calcined at 350°C  for three hours.  A total of 3 grams of the calcined active 
catalyst was taken, shaped into pellets, and loaded into the reactor, where it was activated with H2 gas 
before introducing the CO, H2, and N2 mixture. We collected the gaseous products in sampling bags and 
compared the GC analysis data with standard runs to determine the concentrations of the output gases. 
The normalized molar compositions of the effluent gas were found to be 73% N2, 17.8% CO, 4.5% CO2, 
3.5% H2, and 0.75% CH4. The performance parameters and the selectivity of the catalyst resulted in a 
CO conversion rate of 38.81% and a CH4 selectivity of 0.167 respectively. We collected the gaseous 
products in sampling bags and compared the GC analysis data with standard runs to determine the 
concentrations of the output gases. Our subsequent analysis found potential sources of error that may 
have affected these results, such as inconsistencies in catalyst loading, variable reduction efficiency, and 
gas flow variations.

2.4 Experimental Procedure 
2.4.1 Catalyst Preparation 
The experimental procedure was initiated with the synthesis of the nickel -based catalyst 
utilizing a co -precipitation technique. A mixed salt solution was formulated by dissolving 
11.832 g of nickel nitrate hexahydrate and 65.87 g of aluminum nitrate nonahydrate in 
distilled water. This specific formulation maintained a precise metal oxide ratio of 5:1.7 
Al2O3: NiO. To induce precipitation, 130 mL of a 7N potassium hydroxide KOH solution was 
introduced dropwise into the mixture under continuous agitation until a neutral pH of 
approximately 7 was achieved. The resulting precipitate was allowed to settle, subsequently 
filtered, and washed meticulously to eliminate residual impurities. The solid filter cake was 
then dried and subjected to calcination at 350°C for three hours under atmospheric pressure. 
Following calcination, a total of 12 g of the synthesized catalyst was recovered. From this 
yield, a 3 g sample was extracted, crushed . We did not do any screening as sieve mesh was 
unavailable.  


PAGE 5
 
 
  
 
2.4.2 Catalyst Evaluation 
In the subsequent performance evaluation phase, the 3 g of pelleted catalyst was loaded into a 
fixed-bed tubular reactor. The catalyst bed was first thermally activated at a reaction 
temperature of 350°C under a continuous flow of hydrogen and nitrogen gases. Following 
activation, the methanation process was initiated by introducing the designated feed gas 
mixture into the reactor while maintaining the 350°C operational temperature. The inlet 
volumetric flow rates were strictly regulated at 20 L/hr for N2, 24 L/hr for H2, and 8 L/hr for 
CO2. The resultant product stream was directed through a water-cooled condenser to reduce it 
to an ambient temperature of 28°C before being collected in a tedlar sampling bag. Finally, 
the effluent gas composition was quantitatively analyzed utilizing a gas chromatograph to 
determine the respective performance parameters of the reactor setup.

3. Results and Discussion 
The primary disentanglement of the experimental observation from the ideal methanation 
scenario was seen as early as the catalyst formulation stage. The fundamental objective was 
to drive the reaction of carbon monoxide CO and hydrogen H2 toward methane CH4 using a 
12 g synthesized Ni/Al2O3 catalyst. Following the experimental procedure, the formulation 
was designed for an  Al2O3  to NiO ratio of 5:1.7. While the nickel nitrate hexahydrate was 
precisely measured at the required 11.832 g, the mass of aluminum nitrate nonahydrate taken 
was 66.87 g, which exceeded the theoretically required 65.87 g by exactly 1 gram. This initial 
stoichiometric deviation likely disrupted the optimal dispersion of nickel upon the alumina 
support, setting a precedent for the subsequent anomalous reaction kinetics.   
Following the calcination at 350°C, a 3 g sample of the active catalyst was introduced into 
the reactor alongside an inlet flow comprising 20 L/hr N2,  24 L/hr H2, and 8 L/hr CO2. In an 
ideal system operating at 350°C, the primary kinetic pathway  𝐶𝑂 + 3𝐻2 → 𝐶𝐻4 + 𝐻2𝑂 
dictates a high yield of methane. However, the gas chromatography analysis of our product 
stream revealed a reading opposite of what was anticipated. The normalized molar 
compositions of the effluent yielded 73% N2, 17.8% CO, 4.5% CO2, 3.5% H2, and a mere 
0.75% CH4. Consequently, the system achieved a highly suppressed CO conversion rate of 
only 38.81% and an undesirable CH4 to CO2 selectivity of 0.167.   
In the process of building up a rationale behind this severe underperformance, we can omit 
basic theoretical drift and pinpoint perturbations within the experimental preparation and 
apparatus itself. The discrepancy in conversion and the prominent formation of CO2 over CH4 
suggests that the water -gas shift reaction. 𝐶𝑂 + 𝐻2𝑂 → 𝐶𝑂2 + 𝐻2 was inadvertently favored 
over methanation. 
We can hypothesize a likely cause given the coprecipitation phase: exactly 130 mL of 7 N 
KOH was utilized. If proper steps were not taken into consideration regarding the thorough 
washing of the precipitate, residual potassium ions K+ could have remained on the surface. 
Potassium acts as a severe poison to nickel -based catalysts, drastically altering pore size 
distribution, blocking active sites, and inherently suppressing methanation activity. 
Furthermore, the activation and process fluid dynamics likely contributed to this oddity. The 
catalyst requires rigorous reduction  𝑁𝑖𝑂 + 𝐻2 → 𝑁𝑖 + 𝐻2𝑂 to function. If the palladium 
oxygen trap or silica gel desiccator in the upstream feed were saturated, trace oxygen or 
moisture could have penetrated the system. Oxygen rapidly oxidizes the active elemental

nickel back to inert nickel oxide, while excessive moisture at 350°C accelerates thermal 
sintering, reducing the effective surface area. Finally, the low overall H2 to CO2 ratio (24 L/hr 
to 8 L/hr) compared to the stoichiometric requirement of 3:1 for methanation heavily 
promotes catalyst deactivation via coking (carbon deposition). The formation of these 
carbonaceous scales physically occludes the catalyst surface, arresting the reaction and 
explaining the high concentration of unreacted CO (17.8%) leaving the plug flow reactor.
